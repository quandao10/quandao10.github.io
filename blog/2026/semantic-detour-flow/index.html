Semantic Detour Flow

November 23, 2026

2026   ·   diffusion   flow-matching   pixel-space   generative-models     ·   research  

Quan Dao1, Tuan Pham2, Anh Nguyen3, Duc Vu1, Quyen Tran1, Stephan Mandt2, Dimitris Metaxas1
1Rutgers University  ·  2UC Irvine  ·  3Johns Hopkins University

Diffusion models trained in semantic latent spaces converge much faster than pixel-space models, but they inherit a decoder that caps fine detail. Semantic Detour Flow closes most of that gap without any decoder: it is a pixel-space flow that follows frozen DINOv2 features at high noise and hands over to pixels at low noise. One network, one velocity loss, and sampling that runs entirely in pixel space.

Why pixel diffusion is slow

The gap is in the denoising target at high noise. When almost everything is noise, the best a model can do is predict the posterior mean of the target given the noisy state. For pixels, that posterior mean is a blur — an average over everything the image could have been. For self-supervised features it is a semantic prototype: a representation that still says what the thing is.

So a pixel model spends its early training budget learning to produce blur, while a latent model spends the same budget learning content. That is the asymmetry, and it is the one thing worth fixing.

The detour

Let \(c(x)\) be the frozen DINOv2-B features of \(x\) on the 16×16 patch grid, centred and scaled to standard deviation 0.5 — the scale of the image itself, so neither term dominates. We call it the code. Let \(\Phi\) be the pixel shuffle that maps each 768-dimensional token into the 3×16×16 pixels of its patch, so \(\Phi c(x)\) is image-shaped and aligned with \(x\).

The noisy state mixes the image and the code, then adds noise:

\[z_t = t\,m_t + (1-t)\,\varepsilon, \qquad m_t = \alpha_t\,x + \beta_t\,\Phi c(x).\]

The weights hand the signal over from the code at \(t=0\) to the image at \(t=1\). We use a trigonometric hand-over with a mid-path norm bump:

\[(\alpha_t, \beta_t) = g_t\,(\sin\theta_t,\, \cos\theta_t), \qquad \theta_t = \tfrac{\pi t}{2}, \qquad g_t = 1 + \kappa \sin 2\theta_t,\]

with \(\kappa = 0.25\). Plain pixel flow is the special case \(\alpha \equiv 1\), \(\beta \equiv 0\). Differentiating gives the target velocity, and a single network is trained against it with one unified loss — predicting pixels and code together.

DINOv2-B’s 768 channels are exactly one 16×16×3 patch, so \(\Phi\) is an isometry onto the image. That coincidence is convenient, not essential; other encoders and code widths work too.

Watch the hand-over

The video runs the equation above continuously in \(t\), on a real image with its real code (shown by its first three principal components). On the left is the signal \(m_t\) the path points at; on the right the state \(z_t\) the network actually sees. Near noise the signal is a semantic prototype; near data it is the photograph.

Sampling needs none of this machinery. It integrates a pixel-space ODE from noise to data — no encoder, no decoder, nothing to reconstruct through.

ImageNet

At 256×256, Detour-XL/16 reaches FID-50k 1.43 in 200 epochs; at 512×512, Detour-XL/32 reaches 1.59 in 160 epochs. Without guidance the same models reach 3.69 and 4.19.

Guidance matters, and the two kinds compose. Classifier-free guidance alone plateaus; autoguidance alone is already better at a much lower Inception score; together they are better than either.

Text to image

The path needs no class labels, so it transfers to text-to-image directly. With matched networks it reaches comparable text alignment in 1.5× fewer training iterations than plain pixel flow.

A 3.3B model scores GenEval 0.90 / DPG-Bench 87.8 at 512×512 and 0.88 / 87.4 at 1024×1024 — ahead of the pixel-space models we compare against at both resolutions, while keeping the fine textures and rendered text that a semantic-latent decoder can lose.

Architecture, briefly

A 32-pixel encoder lays out the image and passes its tokens to a 16-pixel decoder that predicts image and code together. The loss is a single velocity objective against targets \(x\) and \(c(x)\), with DINOv2 frozen throughout — it is used to define the path, never trained.
